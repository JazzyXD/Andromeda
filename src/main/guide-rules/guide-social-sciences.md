
# Social and Behavioral Sciences

For psychology, sociology, anthropology and similar courses. These courses mostly ask students to recognize a concept in a new situation, remember key studies, compare theories and connect topics. The core task is a single judgment ("which concept is this?"), not a multi-step procedure. So the guide is built from clear concept explanations with varied examples, and the week quizzes carry the scenario practice.

Follow guide-core for ground rules, exam matching, item writing and how the page looks: plain text by default, boxes only for the content guide-core section 7 lists. This file sets what the notes and practice contain.

## Why

- Studying a definition together with short varied examples beat studying the definition alone when classifying new cases (Rawson, Thomas and Jacoby 2015; an independent replication found a smaller but real benefit). Example order before or after the definition mattered little.
- Examples you're given work better and faster than examples you make up yourself (Zamary and Rawson 2018).
- Classifying examples beat reading or generating them (Steininger et al. 2022).
- Comparing cases side by side beats studying them one at a time (Alfieri et al. 2013, d about 0.50).
- Application-level practice improves application-level exams; fact-only practice doesn't (Agarwal 2019).
- Mixing helps between look-alike concepts (Rawson et al. 2015; Brunmair and Richter 2019).

## Notes

In this subject the explanations carry the weight. Don't compress them to save space; cut boxes, repetition and practice padding instead.

**Concepts (the main unit), written as plain text:**
- The term in bold and a plain definition (1 or 2 sentences that name its defining features), citing the source the way the course does.
- 2 or 3 short, varied examples written into the paragraph, from different settings (everyday life, work or clinic, a study from the course). Use the course's own examples first.
- A "Not the same as" sentence naming the closest look-alike concept and the one feature that separates them.

A KEY box holds only the exact statement the exam may ask for: a definition the materials quote or attribute to someone, or the named parts of a model. The explanation and examples stay outside the box. A concept the lecture simply describes gets no box.

**Studies.** Describe each study the materials name in a sentence or two in the text where it matters: who, what they did, what they found (with direction), what it shows. Use a study table only when a topic names three or more studies that are easy to mix up (researchers and year | what they did | what they found | what it shows), with only the columns the materials can fill.

**Theory comparison table** only when the materials give two or more theories, approaches or models on the same question, compared on the same features (core claim, mechanism, signature study, what it predicts, main criticism). Only the columns the materials support.

**Connections.** A sentence or two where a topic really links to an earlier one or to the course's main frame (for example problem, theory, intervention). Not a section of its own.

## Practice

All multiple choice and true/false practice goes in the week quiz (and the final quiz in a multi-week guide).
- **Scenario classification (the main type).** A new 2 or 3 sentence scenario, then "which concept, theory or approach is this?" with options drawn from look-alike concepts.
- **Study items.** "Which study found X?", "What does X's finding support?", "What would Y predict here?"
- **Compare and evaluate.** "Which finding challenges theory X?", "How do A and B differ?"
- **Connections.** Items that link this topic to an earlier one.
- Put look-alike concepts in neighboring questions so the student has to tell them apart.
- About a third recall (who, what was found, definitions) and two thirds application, comparison and connection, adjusted to the exam's emphasis.
- Every scenario is new, never one of the notes' examples.
- No step tables. A worked scenario, if one helps with a tricky distinction, is a plain paragraph: the scenario, the answer, and a one or two sentence reason.

## In a learning guide

- Each topic: the concepts, studies and comparisons above, in order, as readable text.
- No practice inside topics. The week quiz covers every testable concept and study in the week.
- Short-on-time mode: the definitions, the studies and one example per concept.

## In a study guide

- Core notes: concepts and studies in cue-table form (test-yourself question left, the answer right).
- Comparison tables: theory comparisons and look-alike concepts, only where items share features to compare.
- Methods and worked examples: usually skip.

## Avoid

- Putting every concept, explanation or example in a box.
- Tables for one-description-per-item lists.
- Step tables for single-judgment questions.
- Definition-only practice when the exam asks for application.
- Trick wording, "all of the above" and "none of the above".
