
# Humanities and Reading-Heavy Courses

For religious studies, philosophy, history and similar courses. These courses mostly ask students to know what thinkers and readings argue, use the course's key terms correctly, compare positions, apply ideas to new cases, and write about them. The guide is built from clear argument notes and comparison tables, with retrieval in the week quizzes and writing practice after them.

Follow guide-core for ground rules, exam matching, item writing and how the page looks: plain text by default, boxes only for the content guide-core section 7 lists. This file sets what the notes and practice contain.

## Why

- Self-testing helps in every subject studied, humanities included (Yang et al. 2021, 31 subjects).
- Students summarizing on their own is low value without training (Dunlosky et al. 2013), so the guide gives a structured argument summary and then quizzes it.
- Comparing positions side by side beats studying them one at a time (Alfieri et al. 2013).
- Mixing whole readings doesn't help (Brunmair and Richter 2019); compare them in tables instead.
- Argument mapping looks promising, but the evidence is weak and partly from people who sell the method, so it's an optional aid only.
- For essay exams, practice writing in that format; multiple choice and true/false practice also built knowledge that helped on an essay test (Hildenbrand and Wiley 2025), but doesn't replace essay practice.

## Notes

- **Thinkers and readings** (the main unit), written as plain text: the main claim; the 2 or 3 key reasons; key terms in the thinker's own vocabulary with plain meanings; one objection and the reply, if the materials give them; and how it relates to another thinker or reading. Add context (date, setting) only if the course stresses it.
- **KEY box** only for what the exam expects stated exactly: a definition of a key term the materials quote or attribute, or a thinker's thesis in the course's wording. The explanation stays outside the box.
- **Comparison tables:** thinkers, readings or positions side by side on the same questions.
- **Key terms table** only when the course has many terms that share the same features: term | meaning | whose term | example from the course. Otherwise define terms in the text.
- **Connections:** a sentence or two where the week really links to the course's main themes. Not a section of its own.
- **Optional argument map** for a central reading: claim, reasons and objection as a simple flow.
- If assigned readings weren't uploaded, say so in one line and don't summarize them from outside knowledge.

## Practice

All multiple choice and true/false practice goes in the week quiz (and the final quiz in a multi-week guide).
- **Who would say this?** A short new statement; which thinker or reading fits it.
- **Reconstruct the argument:** what's the main claim, which reason supports it, what's the strongest objection.
- **Compare:** how do A and B differ on a question.
- **Apply:** a new case or example; which concept or theorist explains it and why.
- **Written prompts** (TRY IT box after the week quiz): likely essay or short-answer prompts, with a model outline collapsed right after the box (thesis, 2 or 3 points with evidence from the course, counterpoint).

## In a learning guide

- Each topic: the thinker and reading notes as readable text, and a comparison table where two positions meet.
- No practice inside topics. The week quiz plus one or two written prompts after it cover the week.
- Short-on-time mode: the main claims, key terms and comparison tables.

## In a study guide

- Core notes: thinkers and readings in cue-table form.
- Comparison tables: positions that are easy to mix up.
- Practice: week quizzes and the final quiz, plus timed outline practice for the likeliest essay prompts if essays are examined.

## Avoid

- Boxing whole summaries of thinkers or readings.
- Plain summaries with nothing to retrieve or compare.
- Mixing whole readings together in practice.
- Argument maps as the centerpiece.
- Quotes without their meaning.
- Blending what the thinker said with outside interpretation.
