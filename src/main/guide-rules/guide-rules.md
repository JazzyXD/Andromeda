
# Rules and Standards

For auditing, law, professional ethics, tax rules and similar courses. These courses mostly ask students to apply a rule or standard to a case: spot which rule applies, check its conditions, catch exceptions and reach a conclusion. Worked examples still help here, but as short annotated cases, not numeric step tables.

Follow guide-core for ground rules, exam matching, item writing and how the page looks: plain text by default, boxes only for the content guide-core section 7 lists. This file sets what the notes and practice contain.

## Why

- Worked examples helped both novice and advanced law students reason about cases, with no expertise reversal; less structured tasks take longer to master, so examples stay useful longer (Nievelstein et al. 2013; one study, so treat as limited).
- Comparing cases side by side beats studying them one at a time, especially when the principle is stated after the comparison (Alfieri et al. 2013, d about 0.50; Gentner et al. 2003).
- Practice in the exam's format helps modestly (Adesope et al. 2017).

## Notes

- **Rules and standards** go in a KEY box: the rule in plain words, its source (section or standard, as the course cites it), its conditions as a numbered list, and its exceptions. Why the rule exists and how it's used are plain text around the box, with a short example written into the text.
- **Background and concepts** that aren't a rule (the purpose of an audit, who the parties are) are plain text.
- **Category lists** (types of evidence, assertions, threats, procedures): a list in the materials' wording, or a table only when the categories are compared on the same features.
- **Comparison tables** for rules or standards that are easy to mix up (what each covers, the deciding condition, the outcome).

## Practice

- **Annotated case** (EXAMPLE box, short prose, a few sentences per part): the facts, the rule and its conditions, which conditions are met or not and why, any exception, the conclusion.
- **Contrasting pair** (plain text): two short cases that differ in one condition and get opposite outcomes, then the principle stated after.
- **Fact patterns** (TRY IT box after the week quiz): identify the rule, check the conditions, spot exceptions, conclude. Model answers collapsed right after the box.
- **Multiple choice** (in the week quiz): scenario items whose wrong options are commonly misapplied rules or a missed condition.
- **Written exams** (problem questions, essays): full issue, rule, application, conclusion answers, with a model answer in the key.

## In a learning guide

- Each topic: the rules, the explanation in plain text, then one annotated case for the main rule. Add a contrasting pair where a single condition flips the outcome. Keep annotated cases on later topics too, since examples stay useful longer in this kind of course.
- Fact patterns come after the week quiz, not inside topics.
- Short-on-time mode: the rules, one annotated case per main rule, and two fact patterns per week.

## In a study guide

- Core notes: rules in cue-table form.
- Comparison tables: easily confused rules and standards.
- Worked examples: annotated cases and contrasting pairs for the most testable rules.
- Practice: week quizzes plus fact patterns in the exam's format.

## Avoid

- Numeric step tables for rule application.
- Rules listed without their conditions and exceptions.
- Boxing the explanation or the background along with the rule.
- Cases that only test whether the student can recite the rule.
